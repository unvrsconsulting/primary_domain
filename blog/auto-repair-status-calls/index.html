Is My Car Ready? How to Cut Status Calls at Your Auto Shop

Connor Bolin · Nov 20, 2026 · 3 min read

An auto shop's phone rings the most when every bay is full and every service writer is busy. And a lot of those calls aren't new customers. They're existing customers asking the same question: "Is my car ready yet?" Each one pulls someone away from the counter or from the car they were working on.

An important note: the tools below handle communication only. They don't diagnose a vehicle, approve a repair, or promise a price or a time you haven't set. Those stay with your team.

Why status calls pile up

Most customers don't call because they're impatient. They call because they don't know what's happening, and the only way to find out is to ask. A car is expensive and often their only way to get to work, so silence feels like bad news. When the shop doesn't reach out first, the customer does.

Three updates that prevent most of those calls

  • "We've got your car." A short text once the vehicle is checked in, with when to expect the next update.
  • "Your estimate is ready." A text that tells the customer something needs their decision, and how to reach you.
  • "Your car is ready." Pickup details and your hours. Add a delay notice, too, whenever a job will run past the time you gave.

None of these need to be clever. They need to be consistent. If your shop software can trigger messages at those points, use it. If it can't, a simple template your writers send takes seconds. Either way, only text customers who've agreed to it. We cover that in text message consent for small businesses.

Example messages

  • "Hi Dana, your Civic is checked in at [Shop Name]. A technician will inspect it this morning and we'll text you when the estimate is ready."
  • "Hi Dana, the estimate for your Civic is ready. Call us at [number] to review it before we start any work."
  • "Hi Dana, your Civic is ready for pickup. We're open until [time] today."

What a phone assistant can do

When a status call still comes in, an AI receptionist can answer it, get the caller's name and vehicle, and pass along the status your shop has recorded. If it doesn't have a status, it can take a message and flag it so a writer texts back, rather than leaving the customer on hold or in voicemail. It can also answer the routine questions: hours, location, whether you take a certain type of vehicle, and how to book a drop-off. And if a new customer calls while you're busy under a hood, the call gets answered. See missed-call text-back for how that follow-up works.

What should stay with a person

  • Approving added work or changes to an estimate. The customer should hear that from someone who can explain it.
  • Explaining what's wrong with the car and why it costs what it does.
  • Price disputes, warranty questions, and comebacks.
  • Upset customers. A customer whose car is late needs a person quickly. We write about that in when an angry caller needs a human, not a bot.

Try it for a week

  1. Count how many status calls you get in a normal week. A tally on a notepad is enough.
  2. Pick the points in your workflow where a text makes sense, usually the three above.
  3. Write the messages in your shop's voice and decide who owns delay updates.
  4. Run it for a week and see whether the tally drops.

The bottom line

Customers call because they're in the dark. Tell them proactively at three moments, let a phone assistant handle the calls that still come in, and keep estimates and explanations with your team. See how this fits a full setup on our AI answering service for auto repair shops page.
