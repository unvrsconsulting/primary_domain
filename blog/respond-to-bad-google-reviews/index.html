How to Reply to a Bad Google Review

Connor Bolin · Oct 18, 2026 · 6 min read

A one-star review lands on a Tuesday morning and the first instinct is to either fire back or ignore it. Both are understandable, and both usually make things worse. The thing to keep in mind is that a negative review is mostly not written for the reviewer. It's read by the next person deciding whether to call you, and what they're really judging is how you respond.

We've covered how to collect more reviews. This is the other half: what to do when one of them stings.

The math most businesses haven't run

This one is an assumption, not a measured result, so treat it as a thought exercise:

  • Say about 150 prospects a month look at your Google listing before choosing someone to call.
  • Say two of them, in a given month, hesitate over a visible negative review with no reply, and a calm, professional response would have nudged both toward calling.
  • At an average first job of $500, that's about $1,000 a month, or roughly $12,000 a year.

The real figure could be higher or lower. The point is that the response is a sales asset, not just damage control.

A calm reply framework

  • Reply promptly, but not angrily. Wait until you're calm. A day is fine. A week looks like neglect.
  • Thank them and acknowledge the experience. You don't have to agree with every detail to say you're sorry it fell short.
  • State the facts briefly and politely, only if there's a real factual error, and never argue.
  • Move it offline. Give a direct contact and offer to make it right.
  • Write for the next reader. Assume a future customer will read this, and sound like the person you'd want to hire.

Where AI helps, and where it shouldn't

Writing a thoughtful reply when you're annoyed is hard. This is a good place for a drafting assistant: it watches for new reviews, flags the ones that need a response, and writes a first draft in your voice that you can edit and approve. That saves time and keeps tone consistent, especially across a busy week. It can route new reviews into Slack or email through Zapier, and it fits alongside our Review & Reputation Management service.

Where we draw a hard line

A person approves every reply before it posts, especially negative ones. It never invents facts about what happened, never mentions private customer details (health and financial information are off limits in a public reply), and never offers anything in exchange for a review being changed or removed. It also never writes fake reviews, and it never screens which customers are allowed to leave a review, because both break the rules of the review platforms. Some reviews are better left alone, such as obvious spam or abuse, which you can report through the platform instead.

The bottom line

A bad review isn't a verdict. It's an audition, and a thoughtful response is how you pass it. Fast alerts and a good first draft make that easier, and the judgment stays with you.
