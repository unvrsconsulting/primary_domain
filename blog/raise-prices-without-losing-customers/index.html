How to Raise Your Prices Without Losing Customers

Connor Bolin · Oct 24, 2026 · 6 min read

Most small business owners wait too long to raise prices, and then raise them too little. The reason is almost always fear: a few customers will leave, and the loss feels worse than the gain from everyone who stays. But the arithmetic is more forgiving than the fear suggests, and the way you announce a change matters more than the number itself.

The break-even math most owners haven't run

Here's a worked example, using made-up round numbers, so replace them with your own:

  • Say you raise prices 8%, and your gross margin on a typical job (revenue minus the direct costs of doing it) is 40%.
  • The share of customers you can lose before you're worse off is the increase divided by margin plus increase: 8 / (40 + 8), which is about 17%.
  • With a 30% margin, the same increase breaks even at about 21% lost. With a 60% margin, about 12%.

That doesn't mean you'll lose that many, and in practice most customers lose far fewer. It means a modest increase is rarely the risk it feels like. The riskiest price is the one that quietly stops covering your costs. Your own margin matters here, so work it out with your bookkeeper before picking a number.

Why announcements go badly

Customers rarely mind a higher price as much as a surprise. The usual failure is a new number on an invoice with no warning, or a vague "prices are going up" with no reason. People who feel blindsided are the ones who leave and tell others why. The fix isn't a clever script. It's notice, a plain reason, and a human they can talk to.

A simple way to announce it

  • Give real notice, usually 30 days or whatever your agreement requires, so nobody is surprised by the next invoice.
  • Say why in one honest sentence: your costs went up, and you want to keep the quality they're used to.
  • Be specific about the change: the new price, the date it starts, and what's not changing.
  • Thank long-time customers, and if you want to, offer them a modest grandfathering window.
  • Make it easy to ask questions, with a name, a number, and a quick reply.

Where automation helps

The tedious part isn't writing the message once. It's sending it to the right customers at the right time and handling the replies without losing track. An automated sequence can send the notice by email or text to customers who've agreed to receive messages (see our texting consent checklist), follow up with a short reminder before the date, and route any reply that sounds worried or annoyed straight to you. Where we draw a hard line

You set the price. Automation never picks a number, never changes a price on a contract that doesn't allow it, and never raises a price without the notice you've promised or your agreement requires. The message is yours to approve before anything goes out, it never misstates the reason, and anyone who wants to talk to a person gets one. If a customer pushes back, that conversation stays with you.

The bottom line

A fair, well-explained price increase usually costs fewer customers than owners fear and pays for itself sooner than they expect. The work is in the notice, not the number.
