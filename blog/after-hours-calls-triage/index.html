After-Hours Calls: Which Ones Need You Tonight?

Connor Bolin · Oct 30, 2026 · 3 min read

The phone rings at 8:40 on a Tuesday night. It could be a burst pipe or someone asking for a quote next week. If you answer every call after hours, you never get a night off. If you answer none, you lose the emergencies along with everything else.

The way out is deciding ahead of time which calls deserve to reach you, and what every other caller should hear. Here's a simple way to set that up.

Define "emergency" in writing

Everyone's version differs. For an HVAC company it might be no heat in a cold snap. For a plumber, an active leak. For a restaurant, almost nothing is urgent after close. Some fields, like veterinary and dental, often send urgent cases to a dedicated emergency clinic rather than handling them directly, so decide which referral you'd give.

Write down your list of true emergencies in a few lines. If you can't name it in advance, you'll be deciding at 10 p.m. with a caller waiting.

Sort calls into three buckets

  • Tonight. A real emergency. It goes to a person right away, with a short summary of what's wrong and where.
  • Tomorrow. Real work that can wait. The caller gets the next available slot and a confirmation, and you see it in the morning.
  • Anytime. Questions with a standard answer: hours, service area, what you do. These need no human at all.

What callers should hear

A good after-hours experience tells the caller three things: they've reached the right place, what happens next, and when. Ask the same few questions you'd ask in person: name, address, what's wrong, how urgent. And anyone describing immediate danger to people or property should be told to call 911 first. Your phone line isn't a substitute for emergency services.

Protect the person on call

Decide who gets woken up, and don't let it be everyone. Pick one on-call person, or a simple rotation, and set a backup if the first doesn't answer within a few minutes. Define quiet hours for anything that isn't an emergency. Burnout from a phone that's always on costs more than most missed calls.

Where AI fits

This is a natural job for an AI receptionist. It answers at any hour, asks your triage questions, books the "tomorrow" calls, answers the "anytime" ones, and rings or texts your on-call person only for the "tonight" bucket, with a summary so nobody repeats the story. Your rules still decide what counts as an emergency; the software just applies them consistently.
